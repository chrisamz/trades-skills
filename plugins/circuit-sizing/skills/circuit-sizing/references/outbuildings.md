# Running power to a shed, detached garage or other outbuilding

The script sizes the wire. These are the other things that decide whether the job is right, and the ones people asking about wire gauge most often miss. Raise the ones that apply; this is guidance to pass on, not something the script checks. Section numbers follow the 2020 and 2023 NEC, and local rules often differ on burial depth and on what a homeowner may do without a licence.

## Cable and wiring method

- **NM cable (Romex) cannot be used.** It is for dry indoor locations. Outdoors and underground are wet locations, and so is the inside of a buried conduit, so NM in buried conduit is not allowed either (334.12, 300.5(B)).
- **Direct burial:** UF-B cable. **In conduit:** individual conductors rated for wet locations, such as THWN-2 or XHHW-2 (most THHN sold is dual-rated THHN/THWN-2; check the print on the wire).
- When conductors are upsized for voltage drop, the equipment ground is upsized in proportion (250.122(B)). Cable assemblies come with a fixed, often smaller, ground, which is one reason long runs are usually pulled as individual conductors in conduit.

## Burial depth (cover), residential, 0 to 600 V (Table 300.5)

| Method | Minimum cover |
|---|---|
| Direct-buried cable (UF-B) | 24 in. |
| PVC conduit | 18 in. |
| Rigid metal or intermediate metal conduit | 6 in. |
| Residential branch circuit, 120 V, 20 A or less, GFCI protected | 12 in. |

Deeper under driveways in some cases. Protect cable where it comes out of the ground. Call 811 before digging.

## One circuit or a feeder

- **A single branch circuit** (or one multiwire circuit) to the building is the simple case. It needs a disconnect at the building, which can be a switch rated for the load (225.31, 225.36), and it does not need a grounding electrode at the building (250.32(A) exception).
- **A feeder to a subpanel** needs: a four-wire feed with the ground and neutral kept separate in the subpanel, a grounding electrode (ground rod) at the building connected to the equipment ground bar (250.32), and a disconnect at the building. It is the better choice when there will be more than one circuit, 240 V tools, or a long run where the lights should not dip when a tool starts.
- Only one supply to a building in most cases (225.30): not a second circuit added later beside the first.

## Voltage drop on a long run

- A 240 V feed carries the same power at half the current, so the drop on the same wire is about a quarter in percentage terms. For a long run this often beats upsizing a 120 V circuit.
- Motors draw several times their running current at start. A run that looks acceptable at running current can still give slow starts and dimming lights.
- A larger wire may not fit a 15 or 20 A breaker or receptacle terminal. The usual approach is a junction box at each end with a splice to a short length of the normal size. Check the terminal's marked wire range.

## Protection

- Receptacles in accessory buildings and outdoors need GFCI protection (210.8(A)).
- Outdoor boxes, fittings and covers must be listed for wet locations.

## Permit

A new circuit or feeder to an outbuilding needs a permit almost everywhere, and trench inspections usually happen before backfill. The connection in the house panel is work for a licensed electrician.
